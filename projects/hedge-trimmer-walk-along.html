Walk Along Hedge Trimmer

Traditional hedge trimmers make you stop, put the tool down and rotate the cutting head by hand when switching between horizontal and vertical cuts. It interrupts the job and slows you down.

The brief was a cordless hedge trimmer that lets you move continuously along the hedge, with an integrated mechanism that rotates the cutting head without touching it or setting the tool down.

What it had to do

  • Rotate the cutting head safely, without touching the head
  • Trim continuously by walking along the hedge
  • Reduce fatigue and interruptions
  • Stay comfortable when used upside down

01

Discover

A working model proved the idea. My job was to turn it into a product people would want to use.

The starting point

A model maker built a working model, which was my starting point.

Pulling a release on the centre handle and sliding it back and forth rotated the head up and down. A belt running inside the tube turned a gear on the head.

02

Define

Finding a form that makes the movement obvious, head first, then the handle that drives it.

Initial concepts

I started by sketching the head, to find a general direction I liked. When an idea worked, I sketched what the middle handle might look like with it.

I wanted the design to convey a sense of movement, and to make it intuitive that moving the centre handle rotates the head.

Middle handle development

I started developing the release button on the middle handle, which lets the handle slide to rotate the head.

I moved the release button to the sides of the handle, so you can rotate the head without changing your grip, and built a mechanism to prove the concept alongside the outer surfaces.

03

Develop

The biggest change came here: replacing the mechanical belt with electronics, then redesigning around it.

Key decision

Remove the middle handle and the belt

Who
Decided after discussions with the mechanical designers and the factory.
Change
The middle handle and belt mechanism were removed. Two electronic switches were retrofitted onto the handle, controlling the head's rotation through a worm gear.
Why
  • Cheaper: fewer parts, with no belt and no middle handle, even allowing for the extra electronics.
  • Lighter: again, fewer parts.
  • More accurate head positioning.
  • More intuitive to use.

A dial that mirrors the head

I then changed the control to a rotating dial to make it more intuitive. Roll the dial forward and the head rotates forward. Roll it back and the head rotates back.

A dual trigger means you can use it comfortably upside down. The two triggers are linked, so pressing either one runs the trimmer.

Key decision

Put the safety switch in line with the trigger

Problem
The pull-back safety switch sat on the side, blocking the triggers until released. It worked, but it felt uncomfortable to use.
Decision
After a lot of testing, the safety switch was rotated to sit in line with the trigger. You push it away from you to activate the trigger.
Result
More comfortable, and it reads as part of the trigger, so the handle looks cleaner.

Head and barrel

With the middle handle and belt gone, there was a lot of wasted space in the head. I slimmed it down to house the new worm drive assembly, and reduced the white cover to make it visually cleaner.

There was also wasted space in the barrel around the rotating head, where only the worm gear now protruded from the surface. I sketched a few concepts for it.

In the end I worked with a mechanical designer to reposition the worm gear and shorten the space needed for it and the motor, so the barrel could simply shrink.

04

Deliver

Production surfaces, tooling conversations with the factory, and protecting the design intent through to the tool.

Production CAD

Draft, reflection and texture

I adjusted all the A-surfaces to carry the draft needed for our chosen textures.

I worked with the project manager and the factory to overcome tooling challenges, keeping the original industrial design intact while making sure the surfaces were good enough for production.

Sub-assemblies and packaging

First-off tool

The first parts off the production tools, including the slimmed barrel and rotating head housings, used to check fit, finish and assembly before production approval.
