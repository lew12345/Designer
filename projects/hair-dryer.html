Hair care

Hair Dryer

An ergonomic hair dryer that rejects the generic, clinical form typical of hair dryers. Its shape is drawn from the human body, following the natural curve of the palm to create a grip that feels held rather than gripped.

Organic, ornamental detailing gives it a decorative, vanity-worthy presence, and a rear-mounted convex mirror doubles as the temperature display and control, merging styling and grooming into one fluid interaction.

01

Discover

Asking why hair dryers look like machines, and looking to the body for a better answer.

Rethinking the hair dryer

Most hair dryers prioritise technical performance, which often leaves them feeling clinical and utilitarian.

This concept takes a more human-centred approach, combining ergonomic comfort with decorative qualities so the object feels as at home on a dressing table as it does in the hand.

Exploring forms

I sketched alongside ergonomic research, visual references and studies of the human body, cosmetic objects and decorative artefacts that communicate warmth rather than machinery.

Key decision

The brief changed. The form language stayed.

Context
The project began as a hair dryer with two articulated joints, for hands-free use, easier handling, and fitting several attachments to the nozzle, such as a hot brush.
Change
The brief moved to a conventional fixed hair dryer.
Decision
Keep the form development already done and carry it into the new layout, rather than starting again.
02

Define

Moving from paper to the hand. Physical models settle what sketches can't.

Foam and printed models

Starting with foam, I carved the forms I liked from the sketches, balancing comfort with an organic shape.

The model on the left was 3D printed from CAD built off a carved foam handle.

What testing told us

Testing showed the handle needed more room for the fan and more air inlet at the bottom of the handle. Those two findings set the proportions for the next stage.

03

Develop

Proving the design works, then winning back its elegance with the factory.

First LLWL prototype

A functional looks-like, works-like (LLWL) prototype was built to evaluate ergonomics, balance, overall user experience and performance.

I shared the CAD with the factory so the internal components could be developed in parallel, and so we could start discussing how to house them.

Key decision

Slim the handle without giving up performance

Finding
The LLWL prototype worked, but the form had lost some of the elegance of the earlier models.
Decision
Working with the factory, I redesigned the surfaces to slim down the handle while keeping the performance.
Result
The refinements from testing went into the next design iteration, shown below.

Refining the design

Testing identified several opportunities for refinement, which were incorporated into the next design iteration.

Production CAD

Before tooling, every surface was checked with surface reflection (zebra) analysis for continuity and draft analysis so the parts would release cleanly from the tool.

Attachments

I designed the attachments to match the aesthetics of the hair dryer, so the family reads as one product.

04

Deliver

Validating with production parts, then preparing the product for launch.

EB samples

The factory produced a number of Engineering Build (EB) samples to evaluate the design with production components, validating fit, function and assembly before moving to first-off tooling.

Packaging

Packaging was developed with the factory, covering how the product is presented in the box and the boxes themselves, to give the customer the best experience. Several samples were produced.

First-off tool

The project reached first-off tool, but a change in business priorities meant it wasn't taken into mass production. The design itself is protected by patent USD1099418S1.

Colour

I also researched colour trends and developed colourways for possible brand ambassadors, including celebrities and social media influencers.
